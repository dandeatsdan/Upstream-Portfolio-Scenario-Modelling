## 2. Project Scope, Stakeholders and Success Criteria

### 2.1 Project objectives, scope and stakeholders

The project originated from a senior Upstream finance leadership request to improve the speed and flexibility with which alternative long-term portfolio scenarios could be constructed and compared. Its objective was to develop and evaluate a controlled, transparent and reproducible scenario modelling framework, rather than replace the existing Long-Term Planning (LTP) Plan Book or deliver a production-ready enterprise application. I defined the prototype around the integration of governed planning profiles and controlled manual inputs, translation of financial entities into business-facing modelling cases, configurable scenario operations, and comparable financial and production outputs.

The stakeholder landscape crossed executive sponsorship, planning and finance specialists, data engineering, digital transformation leadership, prospective users, and governance functions. **Table 1** summarises their contributions and the power–interest assessment used to inform proportionate engagement. The principal sponsor and senior planning stakeholders shaped the business mandate, while specialist contributors helped establish the validity and feasibility of the proposed modelling approach.

### 2.2 Requirements elicitation and synthesis

I developed the requirements through a facilitated workshop involving senior stakeholders, direct discussions with planning and technical specialists, and investigation of the existing scenario preparation process. Initial needs were documented and refined through iterative presentation reviews, including sponsor annotations and verbal discussions. This provided a practical means of challenging interpretations without representing the process as formal written sign-off. My previous Upstream finance experience helped me interpret business terminology and identify feasibility concerns, although it also required conscious attention to the risk of privileging familiar assumptions over other stakeholder perspectives.

The approach reflected the human-centred emphasis of design thinking (Brown, 2008): understanding the decisions users needed to make before committing to a technical solution. Ethical practice was equally important. Engagement was scheduled around participants' operational responsibilities; the purpose and use of their contributions were explained; meeting summaries could be reviewed and corrected; and recording was avoided or paused when commercially sensitive assumptions arose. These measures supported informed participation, accurate representation and responsible information handling.

I synthesised the requirements into eight themes, connecting needs such as scenario flexibility, data integration, financial output coverage and traceability to practical design responses (**Table 2**). The complete prioritised requirements register (R1–R22) is reproduced in **Appendix [A]**, allowing the assessor to examine the underlying evidence independently.

The resulting scope deliberately focused on a feasible, testable prototype. Predictive forecasting, industrialised deployment and a validated portfolio optimiser were not treated as completed capabilities. More complex commodity-price and fiscal calculations were explored during development but excluded from the submitted prototype pending sufficient business validation.

### 2.3 Intended outcomes and success criteria

The requirements established that success could not be judged solely by whether the model produced numerical outputs. It also needed to reproduce trusted planning structures, execute explicit and repeatable scenario instructions, make assumptions inspectable, and support informed challenge by expert users. These expectations were subsequently consolidated into six evaluation dimensions: technical capability, business value, governance and transparency, adoption and usability, strategic contribution, and sustainability (**Table 3**). This consolidated framework evolved during the project; it should not be mistaken for a formally approved checklist at inception.

The criteria provided the basis for the controlled testing and stakeholder evaluation discussed in Section 7, including reconciliation against the Plan Book, operation and manual-input tests, usability feedback, and explicit consideration of prototype limitations.
